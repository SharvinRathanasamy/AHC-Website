What is Halal?

The Arabic word halal (حلال) means something that is permitted, lawful, or allowed according to Islamic law (Shariah). Its opposite is haram (حرام), meaning prohibited or unlawful.

In Islam, halal applies to many aspects of life, including food and beverages, slaughtering, ingredients, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, finance, business transactions, and other activities. For food, however, halal involves more than simply checking whether an ingredient is permissible. The food should also meet requirements relating to its source, processing, handling, storage, preparation, and contamination with prohibited substances.

1. Halal must also be Tayyib

The Qur'an repeatedly connects halal with tayyib (طيب), meaning good, wholesome, pure, and beneficial.

Allah says:

“O mankind, eat from whatever is on earth [that is] lawful and good...”
Surah Al-Baqarah 2:168

This verse establishes an important principle: Muslims are instructed to consume things that are both halal and tayyib. Therefore, something being technically permissible does not mean that every form of it is automatically good or suitable.

For example, food should be safe, clean, wholesome and fit for consumption.

2. Allah commands Muslims to consume lawful and good food

Allah further says:

“So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah’s favours...”
Surah An-Nahl 16:114

The combination of halal and tayyib demonstrates that Islamic food requirements involve both religious permissibility and wholesomeness.

3. The Qur'an identifies prohibited foods

The Qur'an also clearly identifies certain foods and methods of obtaining food as prohibited.

Allah says in Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:3 that prohibited foods include dead animals (carrion), blood, pork, animals dedicated to other than Allah, and animals that died through certain improper means such as strangulation, a violent blow, falling, or being gored, with specific exceptions mentioned in the verse.

This establishes that halal food is not determined simply by whether something is considered edible. The source and method by which an animal becomes food also matter.

4. Muslims should not make halal things haram

Interestingly, the Qur'an also warns Muslims against unnecessarily prohibiting things that Allah has made lawful.

Allah says:

“O you who have believed, do not prohibit the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress.”
Surah Al-Ma'idah 5:87

This is an important principle in halal governance: halal and haram are matters established through legitimate Islamic sources, rather than personal preference.

What does Halal mean in the food industry?

From a food-industry perspective, halal means that a product complies with Islamic requirements throughout its relevant supply chain and production process.

This can include:

Raw materials – ingredients must be halal.
Animal origin – animal-derived ingredients must come from permissible sources.
Slaughtering – animals must be slaughtered according to Islamic requirements where applicable.
Processing – prohibited substances must not be used or introduced.
Equipment – production equipment must be appropriately controlled to prevent contamination.
Storage – halal products must be protected from contamination by non-halal materials.
Transportation – handling and transportation must maintain halal integrity.
Cleaning and sanitation – appropriate procedures must be implemented.
Packaging and labelling – information must not be misleading.
Traceability – the company should be able to demonstrate where materials came from and how the product was produced.

Therefore, halal is a system of assurance, not merely a logo on a product.

Simple definition

You could explain it professionally as:

Halal is anything that is permitted and lawful according to Islamic Shariah, while in relation to food, halal also requires the product to be wholesome, pure and safe (tayyib), with its ingredients, processing, handling and supply chain managed in accordance with Islamic requirements.
